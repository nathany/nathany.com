### backstory

At the turn of the century I stumbled upon [E Ink][1], an intriguing type of
ink that can be reoriented to refresh a page with new information. “Minority
Report” exemplified the concept with a future world where cereal boxes play
short animations. At the time, I was contently using my Palm IIIe as both
a PIM and “e-book reader” with [MyBible][2].

Fast forward. My 4-year old Tungsten T has a case of Alzheimer’s disease,
possibly related to the busted power button. Palm has not updated their PDAs
for nearly 2 years, which only makes their prices feel steeper yet. I don’t
consider myself enough of the business type for a Treo.

I do a lot of reading, primarily non-fiction books, including e-books such as
those from [Pragmatic Programmer][3]. If a book is less than 300 pages, I’ll
just buy the PDF. In other cases I have the PDF as a reference in addition to
a paper copy for the first time through.

A PDA can function as a pocketable tool to read books, but at home there is no
advantage over a laptop. I’m unlikely to load a 300 page PDF onto the small
screen, much less a larger book. An E Ink display changes the dynamics… not
only would I want all my e-books on the device, I would be willing to purchase
larger e-books than I have in the past.

### readers

The [iLiad][4] is a geeks dream with an E Ink screen, WiFi and a Wacom stylus.
But best of all, the software is entirely Open Source and there is a developer
community growing up around the device. If I was familiar with Linux
programming and GTK, I’d jump on the chance to port [Sword][5]. While
expensive and already a year old, there is a feel-good sense to supporting
some of the Electronic Paper Display innovators from Philips.

September is to bring [Bookeen’s Cybook 3][6] e-book reader, which addresses
many issues with the Sony Reader. It will be available outside of the US, work
on a Mac, and support open formats in addition to Mobipocket. It utilizes a
second generation E Ink “Vizplex” display with a faster refresh rate and
better contrast than existing devices. The Paris-based company appears to be
custom writing a lot of software with an eye for usability. The proof is in
the pudding, as they say, which leaves me waiting for the initial reviews.

The novel size and nine-button interface is suitable for casual readers of
fiction, and will most certainly lack searching, highlighting and annotation.
I can relegate reference use and studying to my laptop, so putting those aside
my main concerns would be:

  * An accessible and efficient Table of Contents for large books like The
Bible.
  * The ability to view letter/A4 sized PDFs in landscape, fit to width (minus
some margin). This should result in a reasonable zoom level for readability,
and simply paging a half-page at a time. (Ideally pages would be reflowed with
Adobe Digital Editions, but that’s not available yet).

### related gadgetry

Being an Apple user, I am certainly not oblivious to the iPhone. It just isn’t
available in Canada yet, so I’ve been ignoring it somewhat. I did read up on
the [Developer Guidelines][7] and have been especially active on [Laridian’s
discussion][8] of iPocketBible for the iPhone. But that doesn’t mean I’ll get
one.

The [Palm Foleo][9] has been met with disdain, but I actually find it quite
interesting, though not as a mobile companion per say. Like a PDA, your data
is always saved, and with only one application up at a time, the OS breathes a
certain simplicity that a full-blown laptop lacks. Considering the form
factor, rubberized casing, and battery life, it seems like a great little
device for the avid traveller.

Palm OS has in it’s favour the Palm-owned [eReader][10], Amazon’s
[Mobipocket][11], Adobe Acrobat as well as [PalmPDF][12]. Toss in dedicated
Bible and [dictionary software][13] and I begin to wonder why Palm hasn’t
jumped on E Ink. Maybe it’s still to immature a technology? I have never seen
it in person. But if Palm is just too busy with phones, perhaps the iLiad
developer community will take the torch, they’ve already ported a [PIM][14].

### a new paradigm

In thinking about applications for ePaper, I realized we need a whole new way
of handling the UI. Readability and power efficiency are paramount. Rather
than scrolling, we should stick to page flipping. Windows and dialogs feel out
of place.

For data input, I envision filling in a circle with a stylus like those
multiple choice tests in school. Forms with either lines or one-letter per
box. Freeform handwriting would be preferable to an onscreen keyboard, but
takes a lot of processing power unless there is a standard form as with
Graffiti.

Take a page from [Circus Ponies’ Notebook][15], which uses a dynamically
generated index as an alternative to the built in search feature. A Bible
application could have a good old concordance at the back.

A device with a paper display needs software that bridges the gap, creating an
interface that feels like paper, but still has the dynamic abilities of an
application.

I really have a passionate interest in E Ink devices, which is amusing
considering I’ve never actually held or even seen one in real life. The
technology is still relatively young, which makes me wonder if my interest
would hold, or if all this just looks good on paper (pun fully intended).
