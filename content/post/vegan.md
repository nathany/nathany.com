It all began with a potluck.

In 2007 I accepted a summer job to write software for the Human Resources department at Power to Change (then Campus Crusade for Christ). This required moving to British Columbia, and for accommodations, I rented a room in a big house with several other young men as flat mates.

One individual (I'll be leaving out names) was *mostly* vegetarian. He was against factory farming, but wasn't against hunting and fishing. One day we all had a potluck. I decided to make a pasta dish that I had made before, but I left out the meat so everyone could try it.

What I found is I really didn't miss the meat. I had my own homemade sauce and a bunch veggies. I thought it tasted great.

So began my first experiment with a vegetarian diet.

## Vegetarian Experiment #1

I wasn't earning a lot of money while working at a non-profit. In fact, it was half as much as I had earned the previous year. So I decided to try eating vegetarian meals for a month in order to save some money. 💸

One of my colleagues was also vegetarian. Her reasons were for the animals, for her health, for the environment and climate change. Yes, back in 2007.

At the time I didn't know much of anything about nutrition, much less climate change, deforestation, and the like. Before the month was up I discovered that the canned vegetable soup I had been eating contained chicken stock. That's how naive I was -- not even reading the ingredients!

After the month was up, I switched back to my previous omnivore diet. I wasn't yet ready to commit to being vegetarian long-term. It was positive experience though -- the food and the savings.

My misconceptions around protein were part of that decision.

## Vegetarian Experiment #2

It took 7 years (2014) before I switched to vegetarian again.

One of my colleagues was Jewish, and due to the lack of Kosher options, he would eat vegetarian whenever we grabbed lunch.

Another colleague had previously worked in refrigeration before going into computer science. During one of his previous jobs, he had seen a large scale chicken farm in Alberta. That experience was his reason for being mostly vegetarian, but he too was okay with hunting and fishing.

So again there were some vegetarians in my life, and if they can do it, so can I.

This time my reason was weight loss. I figured if I was vegetarian and ate a lot more salad, 🥗 I would loose some weight. The idea has some merit, but it was ultimately ineffective because of all the other foods I left in.

After a few months I gave up and transitioned back to an omnivore diet.

## Unsweetened

It was January 2016, and I was eating the many homemade sweets that I received for Christmas. I also happened to be tracking my weight every day and paying attention. My weight went up and up. This made sense to me, as many years earlier I had cut out soda pop as part of loosing weight. Maybe sugar was the culprit?

I learned a lot this year. Watching lectures, reading books, paying attention to the nutrition facts on food. I cut sugar from my diet and lost 70 lbs.

Whole fruit was okay but juice was out. Ketchup was swapped for salsa without added sugar. And certain foods were just gone from the menu.

By cutting out sugar, I also cut out a lot of fat. Think pastry, ice cream, and so forth.
It was also a year when I developed some social fortitude. Sitting around a table while everyone else ate birthday cake made my dietary choices feel socially awkward indeed. But I was on a streak, and I wasn't going to break it for anything!

So there I was. I felt like I finally cracked the code to a healthy weight, and without being vegetarian at all.

But there was a wrinkle. There was one food that didn't contain sugar but still had a dramatic impact on my weight. If I bought a block of cheese, I would gain weight. If I didn't have cheese, my weight would continue down. So to reach my target weight I had to cut out cheese too.

## Whole Food Plant-Based

That same year I went to California for a business trip with several colleagues, some whom I had never met in person (remote work). We all had breakfast together before getting to work. One colleague ordered avocado toast whereas I had the more conventional whipped butter.

We got to talking about food and over the course of the trip he introduced me to the Whole Food Plant-Based Diet (WFPBD).

I had reached a healthy weight and was trying to determine what to do next. I certainly didn't want to reintroduce loads of sugar or cheese and regain that weight. Whole food meant cutting out ultra-processed foods, which aligned well with cutting out sugar. Plant-based aligned with my vegetarian experiments and leaving out cheese.

It's essentially the restrictions of a vegan diet, but with an emphasis on health.

It resonated with me, so I asked for recipe and cookbook recommendations. We returned to our respective cities, and that Christmas I invited some family over for a plant-based meal.

So I consumed lectures and books written by doctors and fully adopted this diet over the next few months. I enjoyed learning new recipes and never looked back.

When asked how long I've been vegan, I base it on early 2017, but that's not the end of the story.

## Dominion

In 2018 I attended a screening of the documentary Dominion. Whether an animal is raised on  pristine pastures, in confinement, or a combination of both, the last few minutes are much the same.

It's a documentary about the slaughter. And yes, it was difficult to watch. 

There are two scenes in particular that stuck with me.

Due to the nature of the documentary, I am marking some paragraphs as <abbr title="Not Safe For Life">NSFL</abbr>. Skip past them if you need to.

Egg laying hens are separate breeds and industries from the chickens bred for meat. Hens are replaced as needed, which means some eggs are hatched. But what if the hatched chic is male? 🐣 Very few males are needed by the industry. The rest are *disposable*. I find this disturbing in itself.

NSFL: Disposing of the male chics can happen in various ways, but one method is a giant blender that grinds them to bits as the fluffy little guys try to escape. The documentary is filmed predominately in Australia, but after the screening, a person there told me of students studying agriculture in Alberta and seeing the exact same thing.

The film is several years old now, and there has been talk of technology improving the situation by identifying the males earlier on. Instead of being born to a miserable and short existence, maybe the males could remain unborn and become omelettes. Great. 😫

Animal agriculture is the largest cause of animal suffering, but by no means the only source.

NSFL: For me, the most disturbing scene was a little fox having the worst day of his/her life. Skinned alive because it was "more efficient". Presumably so someone can have fancy trim on a jacket. 😫

This film left a mark. It's what shifted my identity from a plant-based to vegan. Extending beyond diet to other areas. I began to consider the materials used for my clothing, whether my shampoo used animal testing, and so on. Minimizing harm more broadly and as much as reasonably possible.

## The Yard

When I bought my first house, one thing I didn't consider is cleaning up the yard. Not only the cigarette butts and litter that people leave, but also the occasional dead animal.

Occasionally there is a thump on the window as a bird flies into it. Sometimes they get back up and fly off. I had owned the place for 3 months when I woke up to a dead pigeon on my lawn. I didn't know what to do. So I asked the Internet, bought some disposable gloves and figured it out. No proper respectful burial, unfortunately, but I managed.

What happened in 2023 was traumatic. There was a stationary shape in my backyard that I couldn't make out from my window. So I went out to look.

> "Oh my God..."

My voice trembled and so did my hands.

There was a dead cat in my backyard.

<abbr title="Not Safe For Life">NSFL</abbr>: It wasn't just dead. It was lunch for something. Ribcage in full view, intestines scattered about. Bugs buzzing around. The poor thing. How long had it been there? How did this happen in my fenced backyard? Was it a coyote or maybe a large bird? Was there a name tag or someone I should let know?

As with the bird before, I found out what I should do, made a plan, and got it done.

But this left scars on my soul. It haunted me. Every missing cat sign, every unrecognizable shape in the backyard. My grass grew longer and longer. I avoided the backyard entirely for weeks, afraid of what else I may find.

Yet that day, in the midst of it all, I found two reasons to be grateful.

* As unhappy as I was about the whole situation, at least it was happening to me, not the children who play next door. Who knows, maybe I do have a father's heart?
* My decision to be vegan years earlier somehow made it ever so slightly less awful. At least I didn't have the carcass of a different dead animal thawing on the counter for a BBQ. That would've been dreadful.

So if you catch me flinching at the sight of raw meat, it's not because of a dietary choice I made. It's because of a traumatic experience.

## Conclusions

That's the 16 year span from omnivore at a potluck to a vegan who can't stand the sight of dead things. It's a significant change but also a lot of time. I didn't become vegan overnight.
